Reading the Surface

What photometric stereo records about a manuscript page, how it does it, and what it cannot tell you.

§ 01What is hard to see

Every codicologist has done this: tilted a leaf toward a window, or run a torch across it at a low angle, and watched the page change. Under flat light a parchment folio is writing on a ground. Under light that skims the surface almost edge-on, it becomes a landscape — ruling and pricking, the furrow of a nib, a scraped correction, the ghost of writing pressed through from the facing leaf.

Raking light works because relief that has no colour still has shape, and shape casts shadows when the light is low enough. Nothing in this document improves on that observation. What follows is about three limitations of how it is usually done.

It is not repeatable. The angle lives in the hand holding the torch. Two scholars describing the same feature cannot be certain they saw it under the same light, and neither can describe the angle precisely enough for a third to reproduce it.

It is not shareable. The observation happens in the reading room, in front of the object. A photograph fixes one angle and discards every other.

It requires the object. Each re-examination is another consultation, another handling, of something fragile.

The turn this document makes

These are not problems with raking light. They are problems with raking light being a gesture rather than a record.

§ 02What HELIO Pro produces

HELIO does not enhance a photograph. It photographs a page several times with the camera fixed and the light moved between exposures, and from the differences in brightness it computes which way every point on the surface faces. From those orientations it reconstructs the relief.

What it stores, then, is not a picture of the page under one light. It is a measurement of the page's surface, from which any lighting can be generated afterwards — in a browser, by a colleague on another continent, years later, with the manuscript back in its box.

The sentence this document rests on

Raking light stops being something you do to a manuscript and becomes something you store about it.

§ 03How it works

A matte surface lit by a lamp is brightest where it faces the lamp squarely, and darkens progressively as it turns away. That relationship is simple and predictable. If the same point is photographed under several lights coming from known directions, its several brightnesses together pin down the direction that point faces.

Do this for every pixel and the result is a normal map: a record of surface orientation across the whole page. Integrate those orientations and you get a height map — the relief itself, in relative units until a scale is set.

Two constraints follow directly, and they govern how a capture must be made:

  • The camera must not move between frames. Everything depends on a given pixel meaning the same point of the page in every exposure.
  • The light directions must be known. This is the whole of the next section, and it is where accuracy is decided.

§ 04Where the lights were

The reconstruction can only be as good as the light directions given to it. HELIO offers three routes, and they are not equivalent.

RouteUse whenAccuracy
1A External lightsA dome of known geometry, or an existing .lp fileAs good as the rig that produced it
1B Sphere calibrationA reflective sphere is in shotBest. Measured from your own frames
1C Virtual domeNeither — a legacy or field captureApproximate; positions placed by hand

The middle route deserves the emphasis. A mirrored sphere placed in the frame reflects each lamp as a small highlight, and the position of that highlight on the sphere is a direct read-out of the lamp's direction. Nothing has to be measured on site, remembered, or carried over from a previous session: the calibration is recorded in the same frames as the object.

The practical recommendation is simply this: include a sphere in every capture as a matter of course, exactly as a colour target is included. It costs nothing at capture time and it is the difference between a measurement and an estimate.

Stated plainly

HELIO's automatic sphere detection is experimental, and the software says so on the panel. It places a circle for you to check and adjust; it is not a hands-off step, and a capture should never be processed without looking at where it put the circle.

§ 05What it finds on a page

Photometric stereo records topography. That makes it suited to a specific family of manuscript evidence — everything that has shape, whether or not it has colour.

Preparation of the page

Ruling and pricking, including blind ruling that left no pigment at all; bounding lines; the pattern of prickings along an edge; the impression of a stylus. These are precisely the features that are invisible under flat light and obvious under raking light, which is to say they are what the method is best at.

Writing, and its removal

The furrow a nib leaves in the surface; scraping and erasure, which thins or flattens the substrate; corrections written over a prepared area. An erasure has a topography even when it has no remaining ink.

Structure and handling

Sewing stations, cockling and distortion, set-off from a facing leaf, losses, tears, insect grazing, the impression left by a former binding.

Impressions and applied material

Seal impressions and their tooling, binding stamps and rolls, and — with the caveats in §09 — gilding and its ground.

The boundary of this list

These are the categories the method is suited to, not a promise about any particular manuscript. Whether a given feature resolves depends on whether its relief is deep enough to measure at the resolution captured. §09 sets that expectation honestly; this section should not be read without it.

§ 06Choosing what to look at

After a solve, HELIO holds a number of different views of the same surface. Treating them as a menu of filters is the commonest way to waste the tool. Each answers a different question.

Two practical points repay their space in any training. The first is that a low light is the useful one: most disappointment with this method comes from lighting a page from overhead and concluding there is nothing to see. The second is that the stack-derived views deliberately ignore the light control, which regularly reads as a fault and is not.

§ 07Measurement, not illustration

This is the section that separates HELIO from image enhancement, and the one that matters most for conservation. The reconstruction is a height field: once a scale is set from a target in frame, it carries real units.

With a scale established, HELIO will report:

  • Depth profiles across a groove, a score, or a loss.
  • Volume and true three-dimensional surface area over a defined region — the latter differing from the flat area by an amount that is itself a measure of distortion.
  • Change against a previous session, by loading an earlier depth map as a reference. This is the basis for monitoring a condition over years rather than describing it in adjectives.
  • Reconstruction quality, so that a reader can tell which parts of the surface the solve is confident about.
The argument for conservation use

A condition report that says “slight cockling, stable” is a judgement, and a perfectly reasonable one. A report that carries a dated depth profile is a measurement that someone else can repeat.

§ 09What it does not do

The credibility of §05 depends entirely on this section being honest.

  • It is not multispectral imaging. HELIO records shape, not chemistry. An erasure appears as a change in the surface; recovering the text that was removed is a different instrument answering a different question. The two are complementary and are often worth doing together.
  • Depth is relative until it is calibrated. Without a scale target in frame, comparisons within a single capture are valid but absolute figures are not. Figure 5 in this document is exactly that case.
  • Shiny and translucent surfaces break the assumption. The method assumes light scatters evenly off a matte surface. Gilding, glaze, heavy burnish and some inks do not behave that way, and the reconstruction is correspondingly less reliable there. HELIO can show where this is happening rather than conceal it, which is the right response — but it cannot repair it.
  • Very shallow relief may not resolve at all. The method finds what is physically there to find, at the resolution it was captured. A feature below that threshold will not appear, and no processing choice will conjure it.
  • It does not replace examining the object. It is a record that reduces how often the object must be brought out, and that lets a question be revisited without another consultation.
On the AI-assisted features

HELIO includes AI tools for description and detection. In cataloguing use these are drafting aids that require verification, and should be described as such. Presenting their output as findings would undermine §05, which is the section that has to be believed.

§ 10What it takes in practice

The commonest barrier to trying this is an assumption that it needs a dome. It does not.

  • A camera on a copy stand, held rigid for the duration of a capture.
  • One movable light.
  • A reflective sphere in frame.
  • A scale target, if measurements are wanted.

Three exposures is the arithmetic minimum; eight is a comfortable working number and is what produced every figure here. More frames improve the solve and cost only capture time.

To be completed from your own studio

Frames per opening, minutes to capture, minutes to process, and gigabytes stored per opening at working and archival resolution. These should be measured on your equipment rather than estimated — a digitisation lead will reasonably disbelieve round numbers.

§ 11Glossary

Photometric stereoRecovering surface shape from several photographs lit from different directions.
RTIReflectance Transformation Imaging — a capture that can be relit interactively afterwards.
Normal mapA record of which way each point of the surface faces, stored as colour.
AlbedoThe surface's own colour with the lighting divided out.
Height mapThe relief, derived from the normals.
Raking lightA lamp close to the plane of the surface, almost side-on.
SpecularMirror-like reflection; the shine.
LambertianA surface that scatters light evenly in all directions. The assumption the method rests on.
